# The long walk

Three tellings of the same walk, each a little different in the memory of it.

## The walk out of town

We left before the light came up, when the streets were still wet from the night's rain and the bakery on the corner was the only window lit. The plan was simple: follow the old rail line out past the last houses, cross the river at the iron bridge, and keep going until the path ran out or our legs did. Neither of us had walked it before. We had a paper map, a thermos of coffee, and the kind of **stubborn optimism** that only works before breakfast.

The first mile was gravel and backyards. Dogs barked at us from behind fences, then lost interest. A man in a bathrobe watered his tomatoes and nodded as if people walked past his garden at six in the morning every day. Maybe they did. The rail line had been gone for forty years, but the town still used it as a kind of back hallway, a way to get from one end to the other without meeting anyone on the main road.

## What the river was doing

By the time we reached the bridge the sun was over the hills and the river had turned from gray to a deep, moving green. It was higher than either of us expected. The rain had come down hard all week, and the water carried branches, a plastic chair, and at one point what looked like an entire fence panel, turning slowly as it went under us.

> A river in flood does not care what you planned. It goes where the ground lets it, and the ground had let it go almost everywhere.

We stood on the bridge longer than we meant to. There is something about watching a lot of water move that makes the rest of the day feel less urgent. My friend said the old railway engineers must have known what they were doing, because the bridge did not so much as hum. I said they probably also never imagined two people standing on it with a thermos, *taking notes*.

## The long field

On the far side the path climbed through a field that someone had let go to seed. The grass was waist high and full of small blue flowers whose names neither of us knew. We argued about it anyway. She said chicory; I said cornflower; we were both, as it turned out later, wrong.

The path through the field was narrow, a single line pressed into the grass by whoever had walked it last. Every so often it split, and we had to choose. We chose badly twice and found ourselves at a barbed wire fence with nothing on the other side but more field. Each time we turned back without complaint. That is the thing about walking with someone you trust: a wrong turn is just more walk.

Things we saw in the field:

- a hare, which froze, then vanished
- two kestrels working the same patch of sky
- a rusted plough half sunk in the earth
- a single shoe, which raised questions nobody could answer

## Lunch at the stone wall

Around noon we found a dry stone wall with a flat top and a view down the valley, and it was obvious that this was where lunch would happen. We unpacked cheese, bread, two apples, and a bar of chocolate that had suffered in the warmth of the bag. The coffee in the thermos was still hot, which felt like a small miracle.

From the wall we could see the whole route we had walked: the town at the bottom, the dark line of the old railway, the glint of the river, the pale smear of the field. It did not look far. It had felt far. **Distance on foot is a different unit** from distance on a map, and I am not sure the two can ever be properly converted.

We talked about nothing much. Her sister's new job. Whether the café in town would still be open when we got back. A book I had been trying to finish for a month. The conversation drifted the way it does when nobody needs anything from it.

## The weather turns

The clouds came in from the west while we were packing up. At first they were the soft, harmless kind, the kind that make good photographs. Then they thickened and darkened, and the wind picked up, and the grass on the hillside began to move in long waves like water.

We had a choice. Go back the way we came, which we knew, or push on over the ridge to the village on the other side, where there was supposedly a bus. The map was vague about the bus. The map was vague about a lot of things. We decided to push on, on the theory that a ridge in the rain is still better than a field in the rain, and that the bus, if it existed, would be warm.

### On the ridge

The ridge was exposed, and the rain arrived properly just as we reached the top. It came sideways, cold and hard, and within a minute we were soaked through. There was nothing to do but keep walking. We put our heads down and followed the line of cairns that someone had built along the crest, one after another, each appearing out of the gray just as the last one disappeared behind us.

It was, oddly, the best part of the day. There is a kind of clarity that comes with being completely wet. You stop trying to stay dry, stop worrying about it, and simply walk. The world shrinks to the next cairn, the next step, the sound of the wind.

## The village and the bus

The village, when we came down to it, was six houses, a church, and a bus shelter with a timetable so faded it might have been written in a dead language. We read it three times and decided the next bus came in forty minutes, or possibly the next day. We sat in the shelter and steamed.

A woman came out of the nearest house and asked if we were waiting for the bus. We said we hoped so. She said it was usually late but always came, and then she went back inside and returned with two mugs of tea, which she handed to us without another word. I have thought about that tea many times since.

The bus came fifty minutes later. It was warm, as promised. We sat at the back and watched the rain run down the windows, and neither of us said much on the way home.

## What I took from it

I have tried to work out why that day stays with me when so many others have faded. Part of it is the river, and part of it is the tea. But mostly I think it is that we did not know how it would go, and we went anyway, and it went fine. Not perfectly. Fine.

1. Leave early, before you can talk yourself out of it.
2. Take a paper map, and do not trust it completely.
3. Carry something hot to drink.
4. When the path splits, choose, and do not mind if you choose wrong.
5. Accept tea from strangers.

That is most of what I know about walking, and a fair amount of what I know about everything else.
