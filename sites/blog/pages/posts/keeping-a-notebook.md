I have kept a notebook in my coat pocket for ten years now. It started as a way to remember the names of birds and became, without my noticing, the place where most of my thinking happens. This is an attempt to say what it is for, since people ask, and since I am no longer sure myself.

The short answer is that a notebook is a place to be wrong in private. The longer answer takes the rest of this post.

## What goes in it

Almost anything, but not everything. A rough list of what ten years of pages hold:

- Names: of birds, of streets, of the person I met at the bus stop who knew about bees.
- Sentences overheard on trains, written down before I can improve them.
- Small drawings of things I wanted to remember the shape of, badly done.
- Lists of books, most of which I never read.

What does *not* go in it is anything I have to find again quickly. Phone numbers, addresses and passwords live elsewhere. The notebook is slow on purpose, and **a slow place is a bad place to look things up**.

### The rule about crossing out

There is one rule, and I have kept it for the whole ten years: nothing is torn out. A page that went wrong gets a line through it and stays. Rereading the old notebooks, the crossed-out pages are often the most interesting ones, because they show the place where an idea turned.

## Why paper

People expect a defence of paper over a phone, and I do not really have one. It is not that paper is better. It is that a phone is a door into every other thing, and a notebook is a door into nothing but itself.

> The palest ink is better than the best memory.
>
> It is said to be a Chinese proverb, though nobody I have asked can find where it was first written down.

There is a practical side as well. A pencil works in the rain, in the cold, and on a train with no signal. It has never needed charging, and it has never once asked me to update it.[^pencil]

## A notebook is not a diary

A diary is written to the self, about the day. A notebook is written to nobody, about whatever was in front of me. The difference matters, because the diary asks *how was today?* and that question has a way of making every day sound the same.

The notebook asks nothing at all. It just waits. When I do go back to it, here is roughly how a year breaks down:

| What                  | Pages | Share |
| --------------------- | ----: | ----: |
| Things seen outdoors  |   112 |   41% |
| Overheard sentences   |    58 |   21% |
| Lists                 |    47 |   17% |
| Drawings              |    31 |   11% |
| Crossed out           |    26 |   10% |

The numbers are rough. I counted them on a wet afternoon in January, which is its own kind of note.

## Keeping the index

Once the shelf of finished notebooks grew past twenty, I started keeping an index in a plain text file. Each line is a notebook, a page and a few words, so that I can `grep` for a bird or a street and find the page it is on:

```text
2019-3  p.14  heron, the canal by the gasworks
2019-3  p.40  "you can't be early for a tide"
2021-1  p.02  first page after the move; the new kitchen
```

It is the only part of the habit that involves a computer, and I keep it that way on purpose.

---

## What it has been for

After ten years, I think the honest answer is this: the notebook is a record of what I was paying attention to. Not what I did, not what I felt, but what I looked at long enough to want to keep. Reading it back is like finding a trail of my own footprints across a field and seeing, for the first time, where I kept stopping.

That seems worth a few pounds a year and a pencil.

1. Buy a notebook small enough to forget you are carrying it.
2. Write in it on the first day, even something useless, so the first page is no longer blank.
3. Never tear out a page.

[^pencil]: A soft pencil, a 2B or softer, for preference. Harder leads fade and smudge less, but they also make it feel like work.
